Load Boards vs Direct Shippers: The Math Small Carriers Miss

October 30, 2026 · Aras, dytch · 6 min read

Every small carrier knows the load board math: DAT’s carrier plans run $59–$339 a month, Truckstop $42–$299, 123Loadboard $39–$79. Cheap. The board fills your truck this week. What it never builds is a customer, and that difference compounds into real money over a year.

What a load actually pays on each route

On the board, you are one of thousands of near-identical sellers bidding the rate down, and a broker’s cut comes out first. Carriers who move the same lane to a direct shipper routinely report 20–40% more per mile, because the shipper’s budget that fed the broker now feeds the truck. On a $2.00/mile board lane, that is $0.40–$0.80 more per mile. A truck running 100,000 miles a year keeps $40,000–$80,000 that used to fund the middleman.

What the board is actually for

Backhauls and gaps. That is the honest answer. The board is a survival tool: it prices in minutes and fills capacity that would otherwise run empty. The mistake is treating it as a growth strategy. A lead on the board is one load at one price with no memory. A lead with a shipper is a place in next quarter’s plan.

Why the direct route feels expensive

Because the cost is time, paid up front: building the list, finding who books the freight, writing, following up, waiting through an 8–12 week bid cycle. Shippers invite three to six carriers into their annual bids, and the list is drawn before the RFP goes out. That work is exactly what a five-truck owner has no hours for, so the board wins by default. Not because it pays better, but because it asks nothing tonight.

The hybrid that works

Keep the board for gaps. Build direct relationships for the base load. Start with one lane you already run well, list the shippers along it, and get your name to their freight decision-makers before bid season (usually Q1 or Q4). Our direct shipper playbook walks the five steps.
