What a Shipper Checks on Your Website Before Answering Your Email

October 30, 2026 · Aras, dytch · 6 min read

You wrote a good email. The shipping manager read it, got interested, and did what every buyer does next: typed your company name into a search bar. What loads in the next thirty seconds decides whether your email gets an answer. Here is what gets checked, in order, and how a small carrier passes each one.

1. Does this company exist?

A real site at a real address, not a Facebook page from 2019. It does not need to be beautiful. It needs to prove an operation exists: your trucks, your yard, your name, your USDOT and MC numbers where they can be seen. Those numbers are every shipper’s first check on FMCSA’s SAFER system; putting them on the page saves them a step and reads as confidence.

2. Do they run my freight?

Equipment and freight type, stated plainly on the first screen: dry van, reefer, flatbed, step deck, Conestoga. A shipper with produce will not email a carrier whose site never says the word “refrigerated”. Name your commodities if you specialize: grain, steel, produce, building materials.

3. Do they run my lanes?

“Serving the lower 48” tells a shipper nothing. Name your lanes and regions: “Midwest regional dry van, Chicago to the Southeast, weekly” is a sentence a freight buyer can act on. If you want dedicated or regional work near your terminal, say where the terminal is.

4. Is anyone actually there?

A phone number that dispatch answers and an email that gets read. Date-stamped content helps: a 2026 photo, a current hiring note. An obviously abandoned site (“check back soon, more coming!”) quietly says the trucks might be just as neglected.

5. Are they safe?

Your FMCSA record is public and the shipper may already have it open in another tab. If your record is clean, point to it. If you run a safety program, name it. Carriers like Hansen Trucking in Illinois publish their SAFER snapshot straight on the site, and it works precisely because it is not marketing.

6. Do they want my kind of freight?

Spot, contract, dedicated, expedite, LTL: say which you want more of. A shipper planning an annual bid skips carriers who look like spot-only board runners. If you are hunting dedicated contracts, the site should say so in plain words.

7. How do I start?

One obvious next step: an email address or a quote form that asks origin, destination, commodity, weight, frequency and pickup window. No mandatory phone call, no ten-field registration. Every extra field between the shipper and your inbox costs you a share of the interested ones.
